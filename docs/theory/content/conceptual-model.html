Conceptual Model & Glossary

This document provides a comprehensive and authoritative reference for all concepts used in SCRIPTA. It defines the core terminology, explains how concepts relate to each other, and establishes the mental model needed to work effectively with the system.

Why This Matters

SCRIPTA is a story specification system, not a story generator. The visual editor creates precise specifications in CNL (Controlled Natural Language) that describe what a story should contain. These specifications can then drive AI generation, be evaluated for quality, or serve as detailed outlines for human writers.

The SCRIPTA Mental Model

SCRIPTA operates on three interconnected layers:

1. Libraries (Content)

Collections of reusable entities: Characters, Locations, Objects, Moods, Themes, World Rules, Wisdom, Patterns, and Dialogues.

2. Structure (Organization)

Hierarchical arrangement of the narrative: Book containing Chapters containing Scenes, with references to library entities.

3. Blueprint (Planning)

High-level story planning: Narrative arcs, beat mappings, tension curves, and subplots that guide the structure.

Structural Hierarchy

SCRIPTA organizes narratives in a strict hierarchy. Each level serves a distinct purpose:

Book

The root container for the entire narrative. A book encompasses the complete story specification including all chapters, metadata, and global constraints.

Book group begin
  // All chapters and global settings go here
Book group end

Chapter

A major division of the narrative that groups scenes into a coherent movement of the story. Chapter properties support pacing, tonal continuity, and chapter-level progression.

Property Description Example
introduction Short introductory text that sets the chapter context Chapter1 has introduction "Anna arrives at the village, unaware of the dangers ahead."
theme Central narrative focus of the chapter Chapter1 has theme "Discovery and Doubt"
start_scene First scene included in the chapter Chapter1 has start_scene Sc1
end_scene Last scene included in the chapter Chapter1 has end_scene Sc5
mood Dominant emotional atmosphere of the chapter Chapter1 has mood "Mysterious, tense, hopeful"
word_count Approximate chapter length in words Chapter1 has word_count 3000
pacing Relative narrative speed of the chapter Chapter1 has pacing fast

Scene

The fundamental unit of narrative action. A scene represents a continuous sequence of events in a specific time and place, with a clear objective and conflict pressure.

Property Description Example
purpose Narrative function of the scene Sc1 has purpose Introduce_Mystery
time Temporal context of the scene Sc1 has time Early-Morning
goal What the protagonist is trying to achieve Sc1 has goal Discover_Truth
conflict Primary obstacle or tension in the scene Sc1 has conflict Hidden_Danger
disaster Unexpected disruption that changes the situation Sc1 has disaster "The village is attacked by bandits."

Scenes still include concrete references such as characters, locations, objects, and actions. The properties above capture the scene's narrative intent and dramatic pressure, and should be applied consistently to every scene (Sc1..ScN).

Scene Change

Scene Change captures what is different after a scene ends. It records concrete consequences in story state, so each scene visibly advances the narrative.

Property Description Example
information_change New information discovered or updated Sc1 has information_change "Hero learns the artifact is cursed"
emotional_change Shift in emotional state of a key character Sc1 has emotional_change "Anna moves from hope to fear"
relationship_change Change in trust, loyalty, or power between characters Sc1 has relationship_change "Trust between Anna and Lucas is broken"
stakes_change How the stakes become clearer, higher, or lower Sc1 has stakes_change "Failure now means exile from the village"

Beat

A story beat is a structural marker in an arc template that explains why a turning point matters in the broader narrative progression.

Scene Flow Units

Scene shows what happens. Scene Change shows what changed. Beat explains why this turning point matters in arc structure. Sequel shows how the character reacts and decides what to do next.

Sequel

A sequel is a reaction-focused unit following a scene. It captures emotional processing, reflection, and decision, linking one scene to the next with clear character continuity.

Scenes are action-focused; sequels are reaction-focused. Together they maintain pacing and make consequences explicit.

Property Description Example
title Descriptive name of the sequel unit Sq1 has title "Processing the Call to Adventure."
reaction Dominant emotional reaction of the character Sq1 has reaction "Fear and excitement"
reflection Character's interpretation of what happened Sq1 has reflection "I must leave now or risk losing everything."
dilemma Competing options or constraints the character faces Sq1 has dilemma "Leave now or stay and protect family"
decision Choice that sets up the next action Sq1 has decision "Prepare for the journey at dawn"
mapped_scene Scene that this sequel responds to Sq1 has mapped_scene Sc3

Design Rationale and Sources

  • Chapter properties prioritize setup, continuity, and pacing (Booth, The Rhetoric of Fiction).
  • Scene properties follow goal-conflict-disaster pressure for forward motion (Field, Screenplay; Vogler, The Writer's Journey).
  • Scene Change and Sequel formalize consequence and reaction loops (McKee, Story; Swain, Techniques of the Selling Writer).

Block

A reusable narrative building block that encapsulates a common story pattern. Blocks are predefined templates for frequently occurring narrative moments like "Revelation", "Confrontation", "Escape", or "Sacrifice".

Block Type Description Typical Use
revelation Hidden truth comes to light Plot twists, character secrets
confrontation Direct conflict between characters Climactic battles, arguments
sacrifice Character gives up something precious Heroic moments, tragic choices
escape Characters flee danger Chase sequences, narrow escapes
reunion Separated characters meet again Emotional payoffs, family stories
betrayal Trust is broken Villain reveals, ally turns

Library Concepts

Character

A person, creature, or entity that acts within the story. Characters have:

  • Archetype: Role in the story (Hero, Mentor, Shadow, Ally, etc.)
  • Traits: Personality characteristics (courage, wisdom, cunning)
  • Relationships: Connections to other characters
  • Arc: How they change through the story
Anna is protagonist
Anna has archetype Hero
Anna has trait courage
Anna has trait determination
Anna relates to Gandalf as mentor_student

Location

A place where scenes occur. Locations have:

  • Geography: Type of place (city, forest, castle, spaceship)
  • Atmosphere: Emotional quality (mysterious, welcoming, threatening)
  • Time Period: When the location exists
  • Characteristics: Defining features
Village is location
Village has geography rural
Village has atmosphere peaceful
Village has characteristic "ancient stone walls"

Object (Plot Element)

Physical items, documents, or artifacts that drive the plot. Objects are often MacGuffins, keys to mysteries, or symbolic items.

SilverKey is artifact
SilverKey has significance high
SilverKey has description "Ancient key that unlocks the forbidden chamber"
Anna owns SilverKey

Event

A specific occurrence that drives the narrative forward, changes story state, and generates reactions.

Events define:

  • Purpose: Why this event happens (plot progression, conflict initiation, revelation)
  • Characters involved: Who is directly affected or participates
  • Impact: How the event changes the story state (information, emotion, relationships, stakes)
  • Outcome: Consequences for characters or plot

Example

Anna_finds_map is event
Anna_finds_map has purpose "Anna finds the artifact map, triggering the quest"
Anna_finds_map has characters_involved Anna, Lucas
Anna_finds_map has impact "The discovery raises urgency and tension in the story"
Anna_finds_map has outcome "Anna decides to begin the search immediately

Conflict

The central tension that challenges characters’ objectives and drives narrative interest.

Conflicts have:

  • Type: internal (emotional/moral) or external(antagonist, environment)
  • Participants: characters involved in the tension
  • Stakes: What’s at risk if conflict is unresolved
  • Resolution attempt: How characters respond

Example

Bandits_attack is conflict
Bandits_attack has type external
Bandits_attack has participants Anna, Bandits
Bandits_attack has stakes "Anna's life and mission are at risk"
        Bandits_attack has resolution_attempt "Anna hides and creates an escape plan"

Event and Conflict are tightly coupled: events trigger state changes, conflicts convert those changes into pressure, and character reactions create the next narrative move.

Mood

An emotional register for scenes. Moods combine multiple emotions with varying intensities to create specific atmospheres like "Mysterious", "Triumphant", or "Melancholic".

Theme

The underlying message or central idea explored in the story. Themes are abstract concepts like "Redemption", "Power and Corruption", or "Identity" that give the narrative deeper meaning.

Story has theme redemption as primary
Story has theme sacrifice as secondary

World Rule

A constraint that defines how the story world operates. World rules establish the physics, magic systems, technology levels, and societal norms of the setting.

World is setting

R1 is world_rule
R1 has text "Magic requires sacrifice"
World includes rule R1

R2 is world_rule
R2 has text "FTL travel causes temporal displacement"
World includes rule R2

R3 is world_rule
R3 has text "Dragons are extinct"
World includes rule R3

Wisdom

Philosophical insights, life lessons, and teachings that the story explores or conveys. Wisdom represents the deeper truths the narrative embodies, drawn from sources like Stoicism, Buddhism, Psychology, or universal human experience.

W1 is wisdom
W1 has label "True strength is shown through restraint"
Story includes wisdom W1

W2 is wisdom
W2 has label "Change is the only constant"
Story includes wisdom W2

Pattern

A reusable story template or master plot. Patterns are higher-level structures like "Quest", "Revenge", "Rags to Riches", or "Voyage and Return" that define the overall shape of narratives. They can include:

  • Master Plots: Fundamental story shapes (20+ types)
  • Twist Types: Common surprise structures
  • Subplot Patterns: Secondary story threads
  • Character Dynamics: Relationship patterns (Heist Crew, Found Family)
P1 is pattern
P1 has label "Hero's Call"
P1 has variable $hero as character
P1 has variable $location as location

P1 has template begin
  $hero is at $location
  $hero discovers "call to adventure"
P1 has template end

Relationships

A connection between two characters that affects how they interact.

  • Type: Nature of the connection (ally, enemy, mentor_student, sibling, romantic, rival)
  • Dynamics: How the relationship evolves (supportive, competitive, conflicted, transformative)
  • Impact: Influence on character arcs and story events
Gandalf relates to Anna as mentor_student
Anna relates to Marcus as sibling
Marcus relates to Villain as enemy
Anna relates to Marcus as supportive
Anna relates to Marcus as conflicted

Dialogue

A structured conversation specification. Dialogues in SCRIPTA are not full scripts but specifications that define:

  • Purpose: Why this conversation happens (reveal, conflict, bonding)
  • Participants: Who speaks and who listens
  • Tone: Emotional register of the exchange
  • Exchanges: Outline of what each character conveys

Blueprint Concepts

Blueprint concepts connect abstract planning units (arcs, beats, subplots, tension) with concrete narrative content (scene actions, dialogues, and events).

Narrative Arc

A predefined template that describes the emotional and structural journey of a story. SCRIPTA includes multiple arc types:

Arc Beats Best For
Hero's Journey 12 stages Epic adventures, transformational stories
Three Act Structure 9 beats General fiction, screenplays
Save the Cat 15 beats Commercial fiction, detailed plotting
Story Circle 8 steps Character-focused stories
Kishotenketsu 4 acts Stories without central conflict
Five Act 5 acts Classical drama, tragedies
Seven-Point 7 points Novels with clear turning points

Beat Mapping

The assignment of narrative beats to specific chapters and scenes. Beat mappings connect abstract story structure to concrete narrative content.

Scene

A concrete narrative unit where story events unfold. Scenes are continuous sequences of actions in specific places and times, and they carry the visible progression of the plot.

Scene Change

A scene-level state delta that records what was modified by the scene events: information, emotional state, relationships, and stakes.

Sequel

A follow-up unit after a scene that captures reaction, reflection, and decision. Sequels bridge major actions and maintain pacing, emotional depth, and directional clarity.

Chapter

A major narrative division that organizes scenes into a coherent section with its own introductory frame, thematic focus, and pacing profile.

Block

A reusable narrative template representing common story patterns such as revelation, confrontation, escape, sacrifice, betrayal, and similar recurring structures.

Tension Curve

A visual representation of emotional intensity across the story. The tension curve plots how much "pressure" the reader feels at each point, typically rising toward the climax and releasing at resolution.

Subplot

A secondary story thread that runs alongside the main plot. Subplots have their own arcs and touchpoints but resolve within the context of the main narrative.

Subplot Type Description
romance Romantic relationship development
rivalry Competition between characters
mystery Puzzle or secret to uncover
growth Character personal development
revenge Quest for retribution
mentorship Teaching/learning relationship

Constraints

Constraints are rules that generated or written content must follow. They operate at different scopes:

Constraint Type Syntax Meaning
requires X requires "Y" Content must include Y
forbids X forbids "Y" Content must NOT include Y
must X must action Y X must perform action on Y
tone X has tone Y Content must maintain tone Y
max X has max prop N Maximum count limit
min X has min prop N Minimum count limit
